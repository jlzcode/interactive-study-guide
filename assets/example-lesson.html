1. Reading the notation

Data. Almost every slide in Chapters 3 and 4 starts from \(n\) pairs of numbers \((x_1,y_1), (x_2,y_2), \dots, (x_n,y_n)\). Think of a spreadsheet with \(n\) rows: \(x_i\) is the input in row \(i\) (for example age), and \(y_i\) is the output in row \(i\) (for example log income).

Sums. The symbol \(\sum\) means "add up".

\[ \sum_{i=1}^{n} y_i = y_1 + y_2 + \dots + y_n. \]

Start with i = 1, stop at i = n, and add up every \(y_i\) along the way.

Worked example

If \(y = (2, 5, 3)\), then \(\sum_{i=1}^3 y_i = 10\), the mean is \(\bar y = 10/3\), and \(\sum_{i=1}^3 (y_i - 3)^2 = 1 + 4 + 0 = 5\).

A model. The slides write

\[ y_i = g(x_i) + \epsilon_i, \qquad E(\epsilon_i) = 0,\quad \operatorname{Var}(\epsilon_i) = \sigma^2 . \]

Each output is a true smooth curve \(g\) evaluated at the input, plus random noise \(\epsilon_i\) that averages to zero and has spread \(\sigma^2\).

The whole of Chapter 3 is about one task: we see only the dots \((x_i, y_i)\), and we want to recover the curve \(g\). We never assume a formula for \(g\) such as "a straight line". That is what nonparametric means.

2. Polynomials and pieces of polynomials

A polynomial of degree \(q\) is \(\beta_0 + \beta_1 x + \beta_2 x^2 + \dots + \beta_q x^q\). Degree 1 is a straight line, degree 2 a parabola, degree 3 a cubic. A single polynomial is often too rigid to follow real data. A spline glues several low-degree polynomials together at points called knots. Lesson 3.1 builds this in detail.

The "plus function" \((x - t)_+^3\) is zero to the left of \(t\) and grows like a cubic to the right. Adding \(\alpha\,(x-t)_+^3\) to a cubic lets the curve change its shape after the knot \(t\) without breaking it. You will see this on lect3 p6 and in the mixed-model slide (lect3 p36).

3. Slope and bending: first and second derivatives

The first derivative \(g'(x)\) is the slope of the curve at \(x\): positive when the curve goes up, negative when it goes down. The second derivative \(g''(x)\) is the slope of the slope. It tells you how the direction is changing, so it measures bending:

  • A straight line has the same slope everywhere, so \(g''(x) = 0\) everywhere.
  • A gentle hill has a small \(g''\).
  • A curve that zig-zags up and down has a large \(g''\), with sign changing at every turn.

Squaring removes the sign, and integrating (taking the area) adds the bending up along the whole interval:

\[ \text{wiggliness of } g \;=\; \int_a^b [g''(u)]^2\, du . \]

At every point, measure how sharply the curve bends, square it so bends in both directions count, then add up over the whole interval.

This number is the penalty in the smoothing spline (lect3 p3). Play with the figure until the next sentence feels obvious: a straight line has penalty 0, and the more a curve wiggles, the larger its penalty.

Check yourself

(a) What is \(\int_0^1 [g''(u)]^2\,du\) for \(g(u) = 3 + 2u\)? (b) For \(g(u) = u^2\)? Answer on paper first.

Show answer

(a) \(g'' = 0\), so the integral is 0. Straight lines are never penalized.

(b) \(g'(u) = 2u\), \(g''(u) = 2\), so \(\int_0^1 2^2\,du = 4\).

Integration by parts (used once, in Lesson 3.2)

The product rule for derivatives, \((uv)' = u'v + uv'\), integrated from \(a\) to \(b\) and rearranged, gives

\[ \int_a^b u(x)\, v'(x)\, dx \;=\; u(b)v(b) - u(a)v(a) \;-\; \int_a^b u'(x)\, v(x)\, dx . \]

You can move the derivative from one factor to the other, if you pay with the boundary term and a minus sign.

The proof that smoothing splines are natural cubic splines (lect3 p20) uses this rule twice. You only need to recognize it.

4. Finding the minimum: set the derivative to zero

At the bottom of a valley the curve is flat, so the slope is zero. To find the value that makes something smallest, take the derivative and set it to 0.

Worked example: least squares with one number

Choose one number \(c\) to summarize data \(y_1,\dots,y_n\) by making \(\sum_i (y_i - c)^2\) as small as possible.

  1. \(\dfrac{d}{dc}\sum_i (y_i - c)^2 = \sum_i 2(y_i - c)(-1) = -2\sum_i (y_i - c)\).

    Chain rule on each square: the derivative of \((y_i-c)^2\) is \(2(y_i-c)\) times the derivative of the inside, which is \(-1\).

  2. Set it to zero: \(\sum_i y_i - nc = 0\).

    At the minimum the slope is zero.

  3. \(c = \frac{1}{n}\sum_i y_i = \bar y\).

    Solve for \(c\). The mean is the least-squares summary.

Every "fit" in this course works this way: write down a sum of squares (plus perhaps a penalty), take the derivative, set it to zero. In Lesson 3.3 we do exactly this with many unknowns at once, using matrices.

5. Vectors and matrices

A vector is a column of numbers, for example the \(n\) outputs \(y = (y_1, \dots, y_n)'\). A matrix is a table with \(n\) rows and \(m\) columns, written "\(n \times m\)". The entry in row \(i\), column \(j\) of \(A\) is \(a_{ij}\).

Transpose. \(A'\) (also written \(A^t\) or \(A^T\)) swaps rows and columns: the entry in row \(i\), column \(j\) of \(A'\) is \(a_{ji}\). A column vector \(y\) becomes a row vector \(y'\).

Multiplication. \(C = AB\) is defined when the number of columns of \(A\) equals the number of rows of \(B\). Entry \((i,j)\) of \(C\) is row \(i\) of \(A\) "dotted" with column \(j\) of \(B\):

\[ c_{ij} = \sum_{l} a_{il}\, b_{lj} . \]

Walk along row i of A and down column j of B at the same time, multiply the pairs, and add them up.

Matrix times vector = many weighted sums

If \(S\) is \(n\times n\) and \(y\) is a vector of length \(n\), then \(\hat y = S y\) is a vector whose \(i\)-th entry is

\[ \hat y_i = \sum_{j=1}^n s_{ij}\, y_j . \]

Each fitted value is a weighted sum of all the observed y's, with weights taken from row i of S.

Hold on to this picture. In Lesson 3.3 the smoothing spline gives \(\hat y = S_\lambda y\), so every fitted value is a weighted average of nearby data, and the weight a point puts on itself, \(s_{ii}\), is the key to the shortcut formula in Lesson 3.4.

Identity, inverse, solving

  • The identity \(I_n\) has 1 on the diagonal and 0 elsewhere. \(I y = y\), like multiplying by 1.
  • The inverse \(A^{-1}\) of a square matrix satisfies \(A^{-1}A = AA^{-1} = I\). It exists only when \(A\) is "full rank" (no column is a combination of the others).
  • Solving \(A\beta = b\) means finding \(\beta = A^{-1}b\). In practice computers never form \(A^{-1}\); they use a decomposition (Chapter 2). The slides say this too: "do not calculate the inverse in these formulas" (lect3 p26).
  • Two useful rules: \((AB)' = B'A'\) and \((AB)^{-1} = B^{-1}A^{-1}\) (order reverses).

Trace. For a square matrix, \(\operatorname{trace}(A) = \sum_i a_{ii}\), the sum of the diagonal. The smoothing spline's "degrees of freedom" is \(\operatorname{trace}(S_\lambda)\) (lect3 p28).

The vector of ones. \(1_n = (1,1,\dots,1)'\). Then \(S 1_n\) is the vector of row sums of \(S\). If \(S 1_n = 1_n\), every row of weights sums to 1, so every fitted value is a true weighted average (lect3 p27).

Sums of squares in matrix form

If \(X\) is \(n\times p\) and \(\beta\) is a vector of length \(p\), then \(X\beta\) is a vector of \(n\) fitted values, and

\[ (y - X\beta)'(y - X\beta) = \sum_{i=1}^n \big(y_i - (X\beta)_i\big)^2 . \]

A row vector times the same column vector is the sum of the squared entries, so this is the residual sum of squares.

Hard point · slow down

Two derivative rules you will use in Lesson 3.3. They are the matrix versions of rules you already know. For a symmetric matrix \(P\):

One number \(b\)Vector \(\beta\)
\(\dfrac{d}{db}(y - xb)^2 = -2x(y - xb)\)\(\dfrac{\partial}{\partial\beta}(y - X\beta)'(y - X\beta) = -2X'(y - X\beta)\)
\(\dfrac{d}{db}\, p\,b^2 = 2p\,b\)\(\dfrac{\partial}{\partial\beta}\, \beta' P \beta = 2P\beta\)

"Taking the derivative with respect to a vector" just means taking the derivative with respect to each entry \(\beta_1, \beta_2, \dots\) separately and stacking the answers into a vector. Setting that vector to 0 gives one equation per unknown. You do not need to prove these rules for the exam; you need to use them.

Check yourself

Let \(A = \begin{pmatrix}1 & 2\\ 0 & 3\end{pmatrix}\) and \(v = (4, 1)'\). Compute \(Av\), \(A'\), and \(\operatorname{trace}(A)\).

Show answer

\(Av = (1\cdot4 + 2\cdot1,\; 0\cdot4 + 3\cdot1)' = (6, 3)'\). \(A' = \begin{pmatrix}1 & 0\\ 2 & 3\end{pmatrix}\). \(\operatorname{trace}(A) = 1 + 3 = 4\).

Check yourself

A 3×3 matrix \(S\) has rows \((0.5, 0.5, 0)\), \((0.25, 0.5, 0.25)\), \((0, 0.5, 0.5)\). Compute \(S 1_3\) and \(S y\) for \(y = (2, 4, 6)'\). What does \(S\) do to the data?

Show answer

\(S1_3 = (1, 1, 1)'\): every row sums to 1. \(Sy = (3, 4, 5)'\). Each fitted value is a weighted average of its neighbours, so \(S\) smooths the data. Here \(s_{22} = 0.5\) is how much the 2nd fitted value depends on its own \(y_2\).

Check yourself

Minimize \(f(b) = (5 - 2b)^2 + 3b^2\) over the number \(b\). (This is a tiny penalized least squares problem.)

Show answer

\(f'(b) = -4(5 - 2b) + 6b = -20 + 14b\). Setting it to 0 gives \(b = 20/14 = 10/7 \approx 1.43\). Without the penalty \(3b^2\) the answer would be \(5/2 = 2.5\): the penalty pulls the estimate toward 0. In matrix form this is \((x'x + \lambda)b = x'y\) with \(x=2, y=5, \lambda=3\): \((4+3)b = 10\).

6. Random quantities: expectation, variance, bias, MSE

The noise \(\epsilon_i\) is random, so \(y_i\) is random, and so is anything we compute from the data, like \(\hat g(x)\). If we could repeat the whole study many times, \(\hat g(x)\) would come out a little different each time.

  • Expectation \(E(Z)\): the average of \(Z\) over infinitely many repeats.
  • Variance \(\operatorname{Var}(Z) = E\big[(Z - E Z)^2\big]\): how much \(Z\) varies from repeat to repeat.
  • Linearity: for fixed numbers \(a, b\) and a fixed matrix \(S\), \(E(aZ + b) = aE(Z) + b\) and \(E(Sy) = S\,E(y)\). The slides use this on lect3 p27.
  • Bias of an estimate: \(E(\hat g(x)) - g(x)\). How far off it is on average.
  • Mean squared error: \(E\big[(\hat g(x) - g(x))^2\big] = \text{bias}^2 + \text{variance}\). The proof is on lect3 new-deck slide 49; you will meet it in Lesson 3.5.
Why this matters

All of Chapter 3 is a fight between bias and variance. A very flexible curve follows the noise (small bias, large variance). A very stiff curve ignores real bumps (large bias, small variance). The smoothing parameter \(\lambda\) (or bandwidth \(h\)) sets the balance, and cross validation chooses it from the data.

Check yourself

An estimator of the number 10 gives 9, 11, 9, 11 over four equally likely repeats. What are its bias, variance and MSE?

Show answer

The average is 10, so bias = 0. Variance = average of \((\pm1)^2\) = 1. MSE = \(0^2 + 1 = 1\).

7. Orders of growth

"Grows like" statements compare how fast things change when \(n\) gets large. If the work is about \(n^3\), going from \(n=100\) to \(n=200\) multiplies it by \(2^3 = 8\). If an error shrinks like \(n^{-4/5}\), multiplying the sample size by 32 shrinks the error by \(32^{4/5} = 16\). Chapter 2 counts operations this way and Chapter 3 states rates this way (lect3 p47).

Check yourself

An optimal bandwidth behaves like \(h \propto n^{-1/5}\). By what factor does \(h\) shrink if \(n\) grows from 1,000 to 32,000?

Show answer

\(n\) grows by 32, so \(h\) shrinks by \(32^{1/5} = 2\). The bandwidth halves.
